Featured Research

Evaluating the Reliability and Validity of a Sensor-Based Device for Gait Analysis

Undergraduate Thesis Research | Ongoing

This ongoing undergraduate thesis project evaluates the test–retest reliability and validity of a locally developed seven-sensor inertial measurement unit (IMU)-based gait-analysis system for measuring spatiotemporal gait parameters.

The study includes 59 participants across three groups: healthy individuals, individuals with stroke, and individuals with knee osteoarthritis. It combines wearable-sensor-based gait analysis with clinical gait assessments, including the Dynamic Gait Index and 10-Meter Walk Test.

Research Objectives

  1. To evaluate the test–retest reliability of a locally developed IMU-based gait-analysis system for measuring spatiotemporal gait parameters in healthy individuals and individuals with stroke or knee osteoarthritis.
  2. To examine the association between IMU-derived gait parameters and Dynamic Gait Index scores.
  3. To compare IMU-derived spatiotemporal gait parameters between healthy individuals and individuals with stroke or knee osteoarthritis.
  4. To compare IMU-derived and manually calculated walking speed and cadence during the 10-Meter Walk Test.

Research Motivation

My interest in this project developed from a broader curiosity about how human movement can be measured and understood beyond clinical observation. I wanted to explore how wearable technology could complement rehabilitation assessment by providing quantitative information about gait and movement.

Working with an IMU-based gait-analysis system introduced me to the practical and methodological challenges of movement measurement, including data collection, processing, and the interpretation of gait parameters. Through my involvement in participant assessment, sensor-based data collection, and data handling, I began to appreciate how measurement reliability and validity influence the interpretation of research findings.

This experience has strengthened my interest in neurological rehabilitation, movement-related neuroscience, and quantitative approaches to studying motor function. It has also encouraged me to pursue further training in neuroscience, where I hope to develop a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying movement, motor control, and functional recovery.
